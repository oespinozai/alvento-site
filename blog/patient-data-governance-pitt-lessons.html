Navigating Clinical Data: Lessons from 'The Pitt' for Healthcare Governance

By Oscar Espinoza, Founder, Alvento, 3 May 2026

The season finale of HBO Max's 'The Pitt' put three governance failures on screen in one episode: a clinician hiding a health condition, a colleague filming patients for social media, and a ransomware attack that took the hospital's systems down. It's fiction, but every one of these scenarios happens in real hospitals. Here's what each one actually requires of a regulated organisation.

Clinician Health Disclosure: Privacy Against Patient Safety

In the finale, Dr. Al-Hashimi has to choose between disclosing her seizure disorder to management or waiting for a colleague to report her. That's the real tension: a clinician's right to privacy against the hospital's duty to keep patients safe.

In the UK, the GMC expects doctors to be open about a health condition if it could affect their ability to practise safely. That's not about punishment, it's about getting the right support, adjustments, or restrictions in place before something goes wrong. The harder question is operational: how is that health information stored, who can see it, and is it ever used for anything beyond a fitness-to-practise assessment?

The policy has to answer that question in writing. Encourage disclosure, define the assessment process, and handle the data under UK GDPR, not under whatever informal arrangement HR has been using. A culture where disclosure feels risky just pushes the problem underground.

Social Media in Clinical Settings

Elsewhere in the episode, a junior doctor is filming videos in the ER during a shift. Real hospitals deal with this constantly, and it's rarely malicious: a background shot of a patient's room or a glimpse of a chart on a desk is enough to breach confidentiality.

In the UK that's a GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 matter, with the ICO able to fine for it. In the US it's HIPAA. Either way, the fine is the smaller cost. The bigger one is a patient who no longer trusts the room they're being treated in.

The fix isn't a memo. It's a social media policy that says plainly what's allowed on a personal device in a clinical area, training that gets repeated rather than signed once, and, where it matters, a secure platform that removes the temptation to use a personal phone at all.

Ransomware: The Threat That Isn't Hypothetical Anymore

The finale's hospital IT department has to surrender to a ransomware attack. Hospitals worldwide have lived this for real, not as a plot device.

The damage isn't the ransom. It's what happens when the systems come back on: clinicians locked out of records and imaging, care reverting to paper, and every one of those manual steps a new place for error to creep in. Patient data doesn't just get exposed, it becomes briefly unusable, at the exact moment it's needed most.

An incident response plan has to survive contact with reality: offline backups that actually restore, a communication plan that doesn't rely on the systems that just went down, and a reporting path to the ICO that's already written before the incident, not improvised during it. Whether to pay the ransom is a real decision an organisation has to have made in advance, not one to debate mid-crisis.

None of this is solved by a security team working alone. Staff training, regular penetration testing, and a continuity plan for extended downtime are the baseline, and cybersecurity has to be treated as a patient-safety function, not an IT line item.

The Common Thread

Clinician wellbeing, patient privacy, and cybersecurity aren't three separate compliance boxes. They're the same discipline applied to three different failure modes: what happens when the record, or the person keeping it, or the system holding it, comes under pressure.

The organisations that hold up aren't the ones with the longest policy document. They're the ones where the policy was actually built for the failure mode it needs to survive, tested before it's needed, and understood by the people who'll have to act on it at 2am.
